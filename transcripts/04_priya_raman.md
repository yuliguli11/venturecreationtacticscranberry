# Transcript 04 — Priya Raman (Early Adopter)

> Synthetic interview. Not real consumer data; must be validated with real humans.

**Interviewer (Q1):** Walk me through your snacking on a typical weekday, from morning to night. What do you reach for, and when?

**Priya:** It depends a lot on whether I'm home or at a client. Monday to Thursday I'm usually in a hotel in Charlotte right now, so breakfast is whatever the lobby has, and by mid-afternoon I'm eating from a bag of Marcona almonds I packed or a Sahale pouch. Evenings are the worst part, because I get back to the room at nine and it's the minibar or nothing. When I'm home on Friday it's calmer, maybe a Rip Van wafer with tea in the afternoon and some cheese while I'm cooking.

**Interviewer:** Tell me more about the minibar.

**Priya:** It's just so depressing. It's twelve dollars for a can of Pringles and a sad bag of M&Ms, and I buy it anyway because I'm tired and hungry. I always feel a little annoyed at myself after.

**Interviewer (Q2):** Think about the last time a snack left you disappointed or unsatisfied. What happened?

**Priya:** Honestly, last Tuesday, the minibar again. I got one of those "gourmet" trail mixes in a kraft-paper bag and it was mostly raisins and yogurt chips that tasted like candle wax. It was sold as fancy, but it was just sugar. What annoys me is the packaging promising something the food doesn't deliver.

**Interviewer (Q3):** Tell me about the last snack you bought for yourself that felt like a treat. Where were you, what was it, and what made you pick it up?

**Priya:** It was a Compartés bar, I think the one with the cornflakes in it, at Happier Grocery a couple of Fridays ago. I was there to buy a hostess gift and I grabbed a second one for myself, which I rarely do. I think it was partly that the week had been bad and partly that I was already holding one. If I hadn't been gift shopping I probably wouldn't have bought it.

**Interviewer:** Why do you say you rarely do that?

**Priya:** I'm much tighter on myself than on other people. Spending twelve dollars on a chocolate bar for a friend feels generous. For me it feels a bit silly, so I need an excuse.

**Interviewer (Q4):** Which snacks have you bought over and over for months? What keeps them in your rotation?

**Priya:** Marcona almonds, for years, usually the Whole Foods ones because they're reliable and not crazy expensive. Sahale maple pecan mix, because it packs flat and it's in every airport Hudson News. And Rip Van wafers, which I order on Amazon Subscribe & Save. What they have in common is that they're boring in the best way: I know exactly what I'm getting, they travel, and they're not too sweet.

**Interviewer (Q5):** Tell me about a snack you were excited to try and then stopped buying. What changed?

**Priya:** Brami lupini beans. I saw them on The Strategist and bought a whole case, and they were fine, just kind of briny and the texture got old. I also bought a lot of those fancy freeze-dried fruit things for a while, the ones in pastel pouches, and then I realized I was paying seven dollars for air. Nothing dramatic happened, I just stopped reordering. That's how it usually goes with me: the first bag is exciting and the third bag feels like a chore.

**Interviewer (Q6):** When you want something sweet in the afternoon or after dinner, what are the options you consider, and how do you choose between them?

**Priya:** After dinner it's almost always dark chocolate, one or two squares, or a piece of good halwa or barfi if my mom sent some back with me. Afternoon is harder, I'll do a wafer or some dates with almond butter. It comes down to what's in the apartment. I don't really go out to buy something sweet, so it's whatever's already in the pantry.

**Interviewer:** Can you give me an example?

**Priya:** Last night we had a Diaspora Co. chai and a Compartés square, because that's what was there. If there had only been fruit I'd have had fruit, but I'd have been a bit less happy about it.

**Interviewer (Q7):** How does fruit, in any form (fresh, dried, freeze-dried or in chocolate), fit into your snacking today?

**Priya:** Fresh fruit mostly on weekends, berries and mango when it's in season. Dates I always have. Dried fruit I mostly think of as an ingredient: apricots on a cheese board, or dried cherries in a salad when I'm hosting. I don't really sit down with a bag of dried fruit, it's usually too sticky and too sweet. Chocolate-covered fruit feels like something you'd get at an airport gift shop.

**Interviewer (Q8):** What comes to mind when you think about tart or sour flavors in snacks? Tell me about any you've enjoyed or avoided.

**Priya:** I love sour, it's kind of an Indian thing. Tamarind candy, amla candy from Patel Brothers, chaat masala on everything, green mango with salt and chili. What I avoid is the American version of sour, which is sour gummy candy that's basically sugar with acid on it. Real tartness makes your mouth water; the fake kind just makes your teeth hurt.

**Interviewer:** Tell me more about the American version.

**Priya:** Sour Patch Kids, those sour belts. Everything is sweet first and then sour on top. I want it the other way round, or with some salt or spice to balance it.

**Interviewer (Q9):** Tell me about the most you've spent on a single snack or pantry item recently. What made it feel worth it, or not?

**Priya:** For a gift, I did a Goldbelly box of Levain cookies for a colleague who made partner, which was about seventy dollars with shipping, and that felt completely worth it because she posted it. For myself it was probably Diaspora Co. saffron, around twenty-five dollars, and that was worth it because I use it for dinner parties. Just for me to snack on, I don't think I've spent more than twelve or thirteen dollars on one item, and that was the Compartés bar.

**Interviewer (Q10):** When you're standing in a store looking at two similar snacks at different prices, what do you look at to decide?

**Priya:** First question is who it's for. If it's for me, I flip it over, I look at the ingredients and the size, and I usually buy the cheaper one unless there's something weird on the label. If it's a gift, I look at the packaging first, honestly: whether it looks like I picked it on purpose, whether it'll survive my bag, whether it's something they've probably never seen. The price matters much less for a gift, within reason.

**Interviewer (Q11):** How do the ingredients, where something comes from, or how it's made figure into what you buy? Give me a recent example.

**Priya:** I avoid artificial colors and anything that looks like it came from a lab, but I'm not strict. The origin story matters most for gifts, because it gives me something to say when I hand it over. With Fishwife I can say it's a woman-founded company doing sustainable tinned fish, and Diaspora Co. is single-origin spices from India with farmers paid fairly, which I genuinely care about. For my own snacks I mostly just want a short label.

**Interviewer (Q12):** Tell me about the last food or snack you gave as a gift or brought to someone's home. How did you choose it?

**Priya:** Sunday, a dinner in the West Village. I brought a Brightland olive oil and a tin of Fishwife smoked salmon with a ribbon on it, both from Happier Grocery. I picked it because the host cooks and I wanted something she'd actually use, not another bottle of wine. My emergency gift drawer was down to a box of Godiva, and I refuse to show up with Godiva. It feels like you grabbed it at the airport.

**Interviewer:** Why was that?

**Priya:** Because the gift says something about how much you thought about the person. Generic chocolate says you didn't think at all.

**Interviewer (Section D concept statement):** "One idea some people are exploring is a line of cranberry snacks made to be eaten as an everyday treat, not just at the holidays. For example, they could use sweet white cranberries or be prepared by hand in small batches, with a short ingredient list and design-forward packaging. I'd like your honest reaction, positive or negative."

**Interviewer (Q13):** What's your first reaction to that? What thoughts come up?

**Priya:** Honestly, my first thought is Craisins and my second is Thanksgiving. Cranberry isn't something I think about except in November and in my mother-in-law's salad. The part that surprises me is "sweet white cranberries," because the only interesting thing about cranberry is that it's tart, so taking that away sounds like making it more like every other dried fruit. As a gift maybe, if the packaging really was beautiful, but I'd need to taste it before I'd believe it's more than fancy Craisins.

**Interviewer:** Tell me more about "fancy Craisins."

**Priya:** Craisins are basically cranberries soaked in sugar. If this is the same thing in a nicer bag at four times the price, I'd see through it immediately, and so would whoever I gave it to.

**Interviewer (Q14):** When, if ever, can you picture yourself eating something like that? Walk me through the moment.

**Priya:** The most realistic one is actually not me eating it, it's on a cheese board when I host, next to the Marconas and the Comté. Cranberry works with cheese, I'll give it that. For me alone, maybe in my work bag in the hotel room instead of the minibar, if it was tart and not sticky. But I can't really see myself reaching for it after dinner over chocolate.

**Interviewer:** What happened the last time you put dried fruit on a cheese board?

**Priya:** I used dried apricots from Eataly and there were leftovers, and they sat in my pantry until I threw them out. So, yeah, that's a pattern.

**Interviewer (Q15):** Which parts of that description matter to you, and which don't matter at all?

**Priya:** Short ingredient list matters, that's the minimum. The packaging matters a lot, but only for gifting. If it's elegant and giftable, like a tin, I'd consider it; if it's cute and pastel with a cartoon cranberry, it goes straight into the "trying too hard" category. "Small batch, prepared by hand" I don't really care about, everyone says that now and I don't believe half of them. "Everyday treat" doesn't mean anything to me, I decide what's an everyday treat.

**Interviewer:** Why was the white cranberry part not on your list?

**Priya:** It's interesting as a story, it's something I could say when I hand it over. But if it's sweeter, it's less interesting to me as a flavor. I'd want a tart version, maybe with chili or something, like an amla candy.

**Interviewer (Q16):** At what price would a package of this feel so cheap you'd doubt the quality? At what price would it start to feel expensive but still worth considering? At what price would it be too expensive to consider?

**Priya:** Under about six dollars I'd assume it's Craisins in a nicer bag. For myself, anything over eight or nine and I'd just buy almonds, I'm being honest. As a gift, it starts feeling expensive but still worth it around eighteen dollars, if it's a proper tin that I can hand over on its own. Over about twenty-eight dollars for a single package of dried fruit I'm out, because for that money I can get a Compartés bar and a Fishwife tin, and people know those.

**Interviewer:** Tell me more about the difference between the price for you and the price for a gift.

**Priya:** For me it's just a snack, so it's competing with the almonds I already buy. As a gift it's competing with chocolate and olive oil, and it has to look like it belongs at that level.

**Interviewer (Q17):** If this were in front of you at checkout next to your current go-to treat, how would you decide between them? What would you give up to buy it?

**Priya:** For myself, I'd pick the Compartés or the almonds nine times out of ten, because I know I like them. I might try the cranberry thing once if it was small and they had a sample. As a gift, I might actually pick it over another chocolate bar, because the host has probably never had it, which is the whole point. I wouldn't give up my real hostess gift for it, but I'd add it on, or put it in the emergency drawer.

**Interviewer (Q18):** How would you think about buying a single package versus a bundle of three? Who, if anyone, would you buy each one for?

**Priya:** A single for me to try, and only if it's small, because I don't want to be stuck with a big bag I don't love. The three-pack is only interesting if I can split it up into three separate gifts, like three little tins for the drawer. For that I'd go up to maybe forty dollars, because that's about thirteen per gift. If they're three bags shrink-wrapped together, I wouldn't buy it at all.

**Interviewer (Q19):** What would have to be true for this to show up in your kitchen every week?

**Priya:** Honestly, I don't think anything shows up in my kitchen every week except almonds and eggs. It would have to taste tart and savory enough to replace the Marconas, be something I could get on Subscribe & Save, and cost about what they do, like seven or eight dollars. Even then, looking at the lupini beans and freeze-dried fruit, I'd probably buy it a few times and drift away. The more realistic version is that I'd buy it a few times a year for gifts, around the holidays especially, which I know isn't what you want to hear.

**Interviewer:** Tell me more about around the holidays.

**Priya:** It's just when cranberry makes sense to people. If I bring it in November it looks thoughtful; if I bring it in June they'll wonder why I brought cranberries.

**Interviewer (Q20):** What should I have asked you that I didn't?

**Priya:** You should ask who it's for, because I'm two different customers. I'm generous as a gifter and stingy about my own snacks, and those are completely different decisions. I'd also ask whether people would still buy it if they had to eat it themselves, not just give it away. And please test the flavor with people who actually like sour things, because to me "sweet cranberry" sounds like you're solving the wrong problem.
