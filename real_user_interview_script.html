Human Validation Mandate

Every synthetic finding cited on this page is a hypothesis, not evidence. The ten respondents in the first run were AI-generated personas answering an AI interviewer. They cannot predict real taste, real purchasing, or real willingness to pay. Their unanimity is itself a warning: all 10 said "fancy Craisins", and real people are rarely that aligned.

This script exists to confirm or kill those hypotheses with real humans. Interviews alone are not enough either. Before any pricing, product, channel or fundraising decision, the founder needs real interviews plus sales tests: sampling, pre-orders with a deposit, and repeat tracking (see Behavioral tests).

Before you start

Recruit, consent, and the rules of the room

Recruit as "a conversation about snacks and treats." Never mention cranberries, the brand, Graza or a product idea in the invite, the screener or the first half of the interview.

Recruiting screener

Ask each item as past behavior and write down the specific answer (store, item, price). A vague answer means the person does not qualify.

  • Store: shopped at Happier Grocery, Erewhon, Whole Foods, Citarella, Eataly or a similar specialty grocer in the last 30 days. Ask which store and what they bought.
  • Treat purchase: bought a treat snack for themselves or as a gift for $6 or more in the last 2 weeks. Ask what it was and what it cost. A receipt or photo is ideal.
  • Profile band: age 25–42; individual income about $100k+ or household $140k+; lives or works in NYC (LA or Miami as secondary cells).
  • Buyer role: a mix of self-buyers and gifters (a food gift given in the last 3 months).
  • Household payers (DMU): include 3–5 partners or household payers who approve or pay for recurring groceries. Where possible, interview both people in a couple, separately.
  • Adoption signal: "In the last month, how many snack or treat brands did you try for the first time?" (4+ ≈ Innovator, 2–3 ≈ Early Adopter, 1 ≈ Early Majority, 0 ≈ Late Majority / Skeptic).
  • Exclude: anyone who works in food/beverage, CPG marketing or market research; friends and family of the team; anyone in a snack study in the last 6 months.
  • Do not screen on liking cranberries or dried fruit. The study depends on hearing from people who don't.

Consent and recording

Read before starting: "I'd like to record so I can quote you accurately. The recording stays with our small team and is used only for research. Your name won't be attached to anything. You can skip any question or stop at any time. Is it OK to record?" If they say yes, confirm again once recording has started. If they say no, take notes by hand. Pay a flat incentive (for example a $50 gift card) and never pay in product, which biases the answers.

Run each session with two people, an interviewer and a note-taker. Allow 45 minutes.

Interviewer rules

  1. No pitching. You are there to learn, not to sell. Don't defend the idea when someone criticizes it. Write the criticism down.
  2. Past behavior over hypotheticals. "Tell me about the last time…" beats "Would you…?". If they answer with "I usually…" or "I would…", ask "When was the last time that happened?"
  3. Neutral probes only: "Tell me more." "Why was that?" "What happened next?" "Give me an example." Accept silence and don't fill pauses with suggestions.
  4. No yes/no questions and no leading adjectives ("delicious," "premium," "healthy," "luxury," "chic").
  5. Record verbatim, especially brand names, prices, emotional words and any unprompted comparison.
  6. Don't mention cranberry before Section D. If the participant brings it up first, note the moment and move on.
  7. Never say "Craisins" or "Thanksgiving" yourself. Those two words are the main things you are counting.
  8. Watch as well as listen. Note when someone pulls out their phone to show a brand, flips a pack over, or checks their bag or pantry.

The Mom Test reminder

Compliments are not data. "That's so cute," "I'd totally buy that" and "My friends would love it" cost the speaker nothing and predict nothing.

Commitments are data. Time (a follow-up call, a tasting), money (a pre-order or deposit) and reputation (an intro to their partner, a friend or a store buyer). End every interview by asking for one. Log whether you got it, and whether they followed through.

The script

Questions Q1–Q20, with field guidance

For each question: the synthetic evidence on the left, and on the right what a real participant has to show or say before it counts. "Green light" and "Red flag" are the signals to write on the debrief sheet.

Section A

Warm-up

About 5 minutes. Build rapport and map their real day before anything product-related.

Q1A1A3

Walk me through your snacking on a typical weekday, from morning to night. What do you reach for, and when?

Probes: "Tell me more about that one." "Where were you when you ate it?"

What the synthetic simulation suggested

8 of 10 described a defined afternoon snack moment, and two independently called it "the danger zone."

It's honestly the most important snack of my day.Sienna Alvarez (synthetic, Innovator)
It's kind of a prop, if I'm honest.Chloe Laurent, on her register grab (synthetic)

What you must validate with real humans

  • Whether they name a specific time and a specific product for the afternoon, or just "whatever."
  • Where snacks come from: a desk drawer, the register grab, the office kitchen, delivery.
  • Unprompted mentions of treats that are "for the photo" rather than for eating.

Green light A fixed daily slot, a named product and a place, e.g. "3:30, a Hu bar from my drawer."

Red flag No fixed slot, or snacking only at weekends. There is no habit for the product to take over.

Q1bNewA1A3

Think about yesterday, or the last workday you remember, between lunch and dinner. What did you eat or drink, where did it come from, and how did you feel an hour later?

Why added: the report's biggest unmet need is an afternoon-slump treat, but the original script never asked about that moment directly.

Probes: "What did you pass on?" "What would have made that moment better?"

What the synthetic simulation suggested

The hypothesized gap is an indulgent but genuinely low-sugar treat that doesn't cause a crash. 8 of 10 raised sugar concerns. Away-from-home options such as office walls, hotel minibars and break-room pastries were described as letdowns.

If it's too sweet I crash and I bloat, and then I'm annoyed at myself for the rest of the afternoon.Sienna Alvarez (synthetic)

What you must validate with real humans

  • Whether they describe a crash, guilt or regret in their own words, without being asked about sugar.
  • What actually fills the slot today (Hu, dates, TJ's mango, a pastry, coffee only) and what it costs.
  • Whether the moment happens at a desk, on the move, or at home. This decides the format (portion pack or resealable bag).

Green light An unprompted complaint about how the current option makes them feel, plus money already spent trying to fix it.

Red flag They're happy with the incumbent ("my Hu bar is perfect"). The need exists, but the slot is already taken.

Q2A1A4

Think about the last time a snack left you disappointed or unsatisfied. What happened?

Probes: "What did you do with the rest of it?" "What did you buy the next time instead?"

What the synthetic simulation suggested

10 of 10 described a chalk-, sand- or wax-like "healthy snack" letdown (8 were protein or healthy bars). The report flags this uniformity as a likely model artifact.

it tasted like chalky sweet sand.Chloe Laurent, on an ~$11 bar at Happier (synthetic)
What annoys me is the packaging promising something the food doesn't deliver.Priya Raman (synthetic)

What you must validate with real humans

  • Whether real letdowns are about taste, price or a packaging promise the food didn't keep. These point to different fixes.
  • What they did next: posted it, returned it, warned a friend, or never bought it again.
  • How varied real answers are. Uniform answers in real interviews would suggest your questioning is leading them.

Green light A vivid, specific story with a brand and a price, plus a lasting change in what they buy.

Red flag They can't recall one. That suggests low involvement in the category, and they won't pay for an upgrade.

Section B

Current behavior & alternatives

About 12 minutes. Probes A1 and A3 through what they actually do. Still no mention of cranberry.

Q3A1A2

Tell me about the last snack you bought for yourself that felt like a treat. Where were you, what was it, and what made you pick it up?

Probes: "What did it cost?" "Show me a photo of it, if you have one."

What the synthetic simulation suggested

Treats were impulse buys at the register, driven by packaging, social novelty or a trusted shopkeeper. Chocolate was the incumbent treat for 9 of 10.

Part of it was that nobody in my group chat had posted it yet, so I kind of wanted to be first.Sienna Alvarez, on chili-crisp almonds at Erewhon (synthetic)
That's usually how I buy things, someone I trust tells me it's good.Lauren Mitchell (synthetic, Skeptic)

What you must validate with real humans

  • Whether they name a real, recent purchase of $9 or more without prompting, and where in the store it happened (register or aisle).
  • Whether they pull out their phone to show the brand, a story post or a receipt.
  • What triggered it: the pack, a friend, a creator, a staff recommendation, or a bad week.

Green light A treat for themselves at $9 or more from a specialty grocer in the last 2 weeks, with a named brand.

Red flag Their only treats are gifts, or the same chocolate from the same shop for years.

Q4A3

Which snacks have you bought over and over for months? What keeps them in your rotation?

Probes: "Where do you usually buy it?" "What does it cost?" "How did it first get into the rotation?"

What the synthetic simulation suggested

Staples stay because they are reliable. 4 of 10 used the phrase "boring in the best way." For 8 of 10, repeat buying depends on a weekly channel (TJ's, Whole Foods, Costco, Target, Subscribe & Save).

What they have in common is that they're boring in the best way: I know exactly what I'm getting, they travel, and they're not too sweet.Priya Raman (synthetic)

What you must validate with real humans

  • Whether any item priced $8 or more is on real repeat, and through which channel.
  • Whether specialty grocers (Happier, Erewhon) are a weekly habit or a monthly treat trip.
  • Whether a subscription is already carrying any of their repeat snacks.

Green light A premium snack bought monthly or more for 3+ months, from a store the brand could realistically be in.

Red flag Every repeat item is mass-market and bought at Costco or Subscribe & Save prices.

Q5A3

Tell me about a snack you were excited to try and then stopped buying. What changed?

Probes: "How many times did you buy it?" "About how many weeks until you stopped?"

What the synthetic simulation suggested

Novelty decay was universal (10 of 10). Buying tended to end quietly, without a decision.

I try things once, and maybe one in ten becomes a regular.Maya Chen (synthetic, Early Adopter)
Then I realized I was basically paying fourteen dollars for gummy bears in a nice box, and once everyone had seen them in my stories it wasn't special anymore.Chloe Laurent, on Sugarfina (synthetic)

What you must validate with real humans

  • The number of purchases and weeks before they stopped. This gives you a real decay curve.
  • Why they stopped: taste fatigue, price, availability, or the social novelty wearing off.

Green light They stopped for a fixable reason (hard to find, the bag went stale).

Red flag They stopped because "everyone had seen it". The pattern is driven by novelty, and a pretty cranberry pack would follow it.

Q6RevisedA1

Think about the last time you wanted something sweet in the afternoon or after dinner. What options went through your head, and what did you end up having?

Why revised: the original "what options do you consider" drew generic "usually" answers. Anchoring to the last occasion shows the real competitive set.

Was: When you want something sweet in the afternoon or after dinner, what are the options you consider, and how do you choose between them?

What the synthetic simulation suggested

Chocolate was the default for 9 of 10 (Zoe prefers sour candy). The choice came down to what was already in the house.

It comes down to what's in the apartment. I don't really go out to buy something sweet, so it's whatever's already in the pantry.Priya Raman (synthetic)

What you must validate with real humans

  • What they actually ate on that occasion and where it came from. Did they buy it that day, or was it already at home?
  • Whether fruit-based or tart options come up at all before chocolate.

Green light The options they list already include a fruit, tart or "lighter" treat.

Red flag Chocolate every time, with a ritual attached ("the part of the day that's mine").

Q7A1

How does fruit, in any form (fresh, dried, freeze-dried or in chocolate), fit into your snacking today?

Probes: "What's in your pantry or bag right now?" "When did you last buy dried fruit, and which one?"

What the synthetic simulation suggested

Dried fruit was seen as low-status, used as an ingredient or as "filler". The freeze-dried raspberries in chocolate at Happier were the exception.

There's nothing chic about a raisin.Chloe Laurent (synthetic)
I've never thought of dried fruit as a treat, it's more like, filler that's slightly healthier than candy.Daniel Chen (synthetic, household payer)

What you must validate with real humans

  • Whether premium dried or freeze-dried fruit (TJ's mango, freeze-dried berries) is already bought regularly.
  • Status language: do they call dried fruit "sad", "a road-trip snack", or "a treat"?

Green light A premium dried or freeze-dried fruit bought for themselves in the last month.

Red flag Dried fruit only ever goes on salads or cheese boards.

Q8A1A4

What comes to mind when you think about tart or sour flavors in snacks? Tell me about any you've enjoyed or avoided.

What the synthetic simulation suggested

7 of 10 liked real tartness without being prompted but rejected candy-sour ("sugar with acid"). 3 feared it would be too tart or bitter. This split matters for the choice between a tart product and a sweet white cranberry.

Real tartness makes your mouth water; the fake kind just makes your teeth hurt.Priya Raman (synthetic)

What you must validate with real humans

  • The specific tart products they name (sour cherry, li hing mui, amla, yuzu, sour strips) and whether they buy them.
  • Which segments lean tart and which avoid it. Log this against the concept variant in Section D.

Green light They name a tart or salty-tart product they buy repeatedly.

Red flag "Sour isn't for me". Record this so you can check it against their reaction to the tart variant.

Section C

Values, price & signals

About 10 minutes. Probes A2 and A4, plus gifting and household decisions.

Q9RevisedA2

Tell me about the most you've spent recently on a snack for yourself to eat, not a gift and not a pantry staple. What made it feel worth it, or not?

Why revised: synthetic answers mixed up olive oil ($40 Brightland), gifts ($70 Levain box) and $65 pralinés, which inflated the price anchors. Separate them. Ask about gifts at Q12.

Was: Tell me about the most you've spent on a single snack or pantry item recently…

What the synthetic simulation suggested

True self-snack ceilings clustered well below the $9–$16 band. Regret, not the price itself, was what hurt.

Just for me to snack on, I don't think I've spent more than twelve or thirteen dollars on one item, and that was the Compartés bar.Priya Raman (synthetic)
It's not really about the number, it's about whether I feel dumb afterward.Chloe Laurent (synthetic)

What you must validate with real humans

  • The actual dollar figure and item, which is the real anchor for Q16. Write it down.
  • Whether they felt regret or "dumb" afterward, and whether they bought it again.

Green light A self-snack purchase of $12 or more in the last month, with no regret and a repeat buy.

Red flag The self-snack ceiling is under $8. A $10+ single will be a gift-only product for this person.

Q10A2A4

When you're standing in a store looking at two similar snacks at different prices, what do you look at to decide?

Probe: "How much more would you pay for the one you prefer?"

What the synthetic simulation suggested

Packaging came first for the aesthetic buyers, unit price for the payer. The premium for "cute" was small.

Like, two or three dollars. If it's double, I need a reason besides a bow on the box.Chloe Laurent (synthetic)
The unit price on the shelf tag, first thing. Per ounce.Daniel Chen (synthetic)

What you must validate with real humans

  • The dollar premium they will accept for a better-looking pack.
  • The order in which they check things: pack, then back label, then price, or something else.
  • For store intercepts, watch what they actually pick up and put back.

Green light They describe paying about double for a specific product because of a reason they can say out loud.

Red flag "I get the cheaper one if both look fine." Premium positioning alone won't move them.

Q10bNewA4A1

Tell me about the last time you turned a snack over in the store and put it back. What did you see?

Why added: sugar and label concerns came up in 8 of 10 synthetic interviews, but only through side comments. This probe captures the actual rule they use.

Probes: "Which number did you look at?" "What would it have needed to say?"

What the synthetic simulation suggested

7 of 10 would turn the pack over and check sugar first. Only Olivia asked for a front-of-pack sugar number (under 5 g).

If the more expensive one is just prettier and has the same sugar, I get genuinely irritated, that's where I feel scammed.Olivia Brooks (synthetic)
Because paying extra for clean feels like an investment, paying extra for sugar feels like being played.Olivia Brooks (synthetic)

What you must validate with real humans

  • Whether they actually turn the pack over (observe it in store, or ask for a specific recent product).
  • Their threshold: added sugar or total sugar, a gram figure, whether sugar is the first ingredient, seed oils, dyes.
  • Whether provenance or farm information ever gets read.

Green light They name a gram threshold or an ingredient rule without being prompted. A clean label can work as a gate and as a message.

Red flag Nobody reads labels in your sample, so a "short ingredient list" won't set the product apart.

Q11A4

How do the ingredients, where something comes from, or how it's made figure into what you buy? Give me a recent example.

What the synthetic simulation suggested

"Small batch" was dismissed as noise by all 10. Specific provenance, such as a named farm or maker, carried weight.

I want something specific, like a named farm or a real reason the process matters.Sienna Alvarez (synthetic)

What you must validate with real humans

  • Whether they can retell a brand's origin story they've passed on to someone (Fishwife, Diaspora Co., Graza).
  • Which claims they now ignore, in their own words.

Green light They retell a specific provenance story without being asked. A named-grower story could earn a premium.

Red flag Every sourcing claim sounds like marketing to them. Put the investment into taste instead.

Q12RevisedA2A3

Tell me about the last food or snack you gave as a gift or brought to someone's home. How did you choose it, what did it cost, and what month was it?

Why revised: the stated price for gifts was roughly double the price for self-purchase, and cranberry gifting tied strongly to November and December. Asking for the price and the month captures both.

Was: Tell me about the last food or snack you gave as a gift or brought to someone's home. How did you choose it?

What the synthetic simulation suggested

Gift WTP roughly doubled: Priya went from $8–9 for herself to $18 as a gift, and Daniel from $6 to $15. All 5 respondents at $24+ for a trio were buying it as a gift.

Because the gift moment is the unboxing. Nobody remembers what the gummy bear tasted like.Chloe Laurent (synthetic)
If I bring it in November it looks thoughtful; if I bring it in June they'll wonder why I brought cranberries.Priya Raman (synthetic; said after Section D)

What you must validate with real humans

  • The real price and the month of their last three food gifts.
  • What they did last Thanksgiving compared with last Tuesday. Is gifting food a year-round habit or a holiday one?
  • Where they buy gifts (Happier, Eataly, Goldbelly) and whether they keep an "emergency gift drawer."

Green light They give a $20+ food gift at least monthly, including months from February to August.

Red flag Food gifting happens only between November and December, which makes it a seasonal business.

Q12bNewA4A2

What's something you would never bring as a gift to someone's home? What makes it wrong?

Why added: the "anti-Godiva" hostess-gift signal was the strongest runner-up opportunity, and it surfaced only by accident.

What the synthetic simulation suggested

I refuse to show up with Godiva. It feels like you grabbed it at the airport.Priya Raman (synthetic)
The worst outcome is spending $40 on something that looks like I grabbed it at a CVS.Daniel Chen (synthetic)

What you must validate with real humans

  • The specific brands they rule out and why: too generic, too "airport", or too cute.
  • The qualities a gift must have: shows thought, travels well, is a tin you keep, is something the host has never seen.

Green light Clear rules for gifts plus a current go-to they would like to replace. That is an opening for a gift tin.

Red flag "Wine or flowers, always." There is no food-gift slot to win.

Q12cNewA3A2

Who else in your household has a say in which snacks get bought again? Walk me through the last time something was added to, or dropped from, the regular list.

Why added: the household decision-making unit only appeared through the one synthetic payer. Recurring purchases may need a second person's approval.

Probes: "Who pays for the things you buy every week?" "Who orders the subscriptions, and who cancels them?"

What the synthetic simulation suggested

2 of 10 named a household gate. Daniel's partner decides on taste and he decides on price per ounce. Rachel's kids decide what goes on the list.

So if you want it to be regular, you have to get past both of us, her on taste and me on price per ounce.Daniel Chen (synthetic)
if they won't eat it, it doesn't matter what I think.Rachel Goldberg (synthetic)

What you must validate with real humans

  • Who pays for recurring purchases and who can cancel them.
  • Whether the treat is bought on a separate personal budget ("my Happier money") or from the household grocery budget.
  • Ask to interview the partner or payer separately. Log it as a reputation commitment.

Green light She controls her own treat spending, or the payer already approves premium repeat items.

Red flag A payer reviews recurring items per ounce and cancels anything that piles up.

Section D

Concept exposure: rotate one variant per interview

About 8 minutes. Read exactly one variant word for word. Rotate A → B → C across interviews and write the variant letter on the debrief sheet. Don't show the other variants.

Revised Why: the original statement bundled "sweet white cranberries," "by hand in small batches," "everyday treat" and "design-forward packaging." It produced claim fatigue in all 10 synthetic respondents: 10 dismissed "small batch" and 8 doubted "everyday treat". For 4 of them, "sweet" was the wrong direction. Each variant now tests one idea and carries no marketing claims.

Was: "One idea some people are exploring is a line of cranberry snacks made to be eaten as an everyday treat, not just at the holidays. For example, they could use sweet white cranberries or be prepared by hand in small batches, with a short ingredient list and design-forward packaging. I'd like your honest reaction, positive or negative."

Opening line for every variant: "I'm going to describe an idea some people are exploring. There's no right answer, and I didn't design it, so please be honest, positive or negative."

Variant A · Tart-forward, low sugar

"A dried cranberry snack made to taste tart rather than sweet, with the grams of added sugar printed on the front. It comes in a small bag meant for an afternoon snack."

Tests: the afternoon-slump need (A1, A3) and the sugar gate (A4).

Variant B · White cranberry

"A snack made from white cranberries, which are harvested before they turn red and taste milder than red ones. It comes in a small bag."

Tests: whether the one element that sparked curiosity in the synthetic run (9/10 called it new) creates desire and not just curiosity (A1, A4).

Variant C · Gift tin

"A cranberry snack sold in a small tin, meant to be brought as a gift to someone's home or put out when you're hosting."

Tests: the "anti-Godiva" hostess-gift opening and whether gift demand holds outside the holidays (A2, A3).

If you have product: run Q13–Q15 on the verbal concept first, then offer a tasting and record their reaction word for word before moving on to Q16. Tasting was the biggest thing missing from the synthetic run.

Q13RevisedA1A4

What's your first reaction to that? What does it remind you of?

Why revised: "What does it remind you of?" draws out the category frame they reach for on their own. Count it per variant. The interviewer must never say "Craisins."

Was: What's your first reaction to that? What thoughts come up?

What the synthetic simulation suggested

10 of 10 said "fancy Craisins" without prompting, and 10 of 10 raised Thanksgiving. White cranberry was the only element that sparked curiosity (9 of 10).

Honestly my first thought is, is this just fancy Craisins?Chloe Laurent (synthetic)
And I'd ask people like me whether cranberry can ever escape Thanksgiving, because I'm not sure it can.Lauren Mitchell (synthetic)

What you must validate with real humans

  • The unprompted "Craisins" and "Thanksgiving" rate for each variant. Tally them on the debrief sheet.
  • Body language: leaning in, a pause, a laugh, or a flat "hm."
  • Questions they ask without being prompted, such as "Where can I get it?" or "What does it cost?"

Green light They ask where to buy it, or compare it to something they love that isn't Craisins.

Red flag "Craisins" comes up for all three variants, so the frame survives every positioning.

Q14RevisedA1A3

Think back to the day you described at the start. Where, if anywhere, would something like this have fit? Walk me through that moment.

Why revised: the open "when could you picture…" led 8 of 10 straight to a cheese board or party. Anchoring to their real day from Q1 and Q1b tests the everyday slot.

Was: When, if ever, can you picture yourself eating something like that? Walk me through the moment.

What the synthetic simulation suggested

8 of 10 pictured a board or party first. 6 could picture a personal moment, and every one of them attached a condition.

If it came in little portion packs, I could see it being in my drawer as a three o'clock thing, instead of the pastry.Hannah Kim (synthetic)
The most realistic one is actually not me eating it, it's on a cheese board when I host, next to the Marconas and the Comté.Priya Raman (synthetic)

What you must validate with real humans

  • Whether the moment they name matches a real slot from Q1 or Q1b, and which named product it would replace.
  • The conditions they attach (sugar, format, tartness). Log each one word for word.

Green light They put it in an existing weekday slot and name what it replaces.

Red flag The only moment they name is hosting, a board or a holiday.

Q15A4

Which parts of that description matter to you, and which don't matter at all?

If you have packaging mockups, show them only after this answer (for example cute pastel, an understated tin, clinical minimal) and ask, "Which of these would you pick up, and why?"

What the synthetic simulation suggested

Short ingredient list mattered to 8 of 10, as a gate. Packaging mattered to 9, but 0 said it would drive a repeat purchase. 3 rejected "cute" outright, which is a direct challenge to the founder's "cute chic" brief.

If it's elegant and giftable, like a tin, I'd consider it; if it's cute and pastel with a cartoon cranberry, it goes straight into the "trying too hard" category.Priya Raman (synthetic)

What you must validate with real humans

  • Whether "cute" helps or hurts with the real beachhead woman, and whether that differs between self-buying and gifting.
  • Which element they bring up first, and which one they mention again later without prompting.

Green light They pick up a mockup, photograph it, or ask to keep it.

Red flag "Paid for the wrapper" language, or "cute" read as juvenile.

Section E

Closing: willingness to pay, trade-offs & commitment

About 10 minutes. End on a concrete ask, not a compliment.

Q16RevisedA2

Picture a bag this size [show a 3 oz / 85 g mockup or hold up your hand]. Buying it for yourself, at what price would it feel so cheap you'd doubt the quality? At what price would it start to feel expensive but still worth considering? At what price would it be too expensive to consider? Now answer the same three for a gift.

Why revised: without a stated size the answers had no anchor ("Depends on the ounces, I'd need to know." Daniel Chen, synthetic), and self and gift prices got mixed together. Pack size is now fixed, and self and gift are asked separately.

Was: At what price would a package of this feel so cheap… expensive but still worth considering… too expensive to consider?

What the synthetic simulation suggested

  • Too cheap: median $4
  • Self expensive-but-considering: median $8 (mean $8.50). Only 4 of 10 were at $9 or more.
  • Self too-expensive: median $12.50. 8 of 10 were at $16 or less.
  • Stated behavior: 7 try once, 3 occasional gift, 0 recurring.

Run in parallel

Behavioral tests

Interviews explain why. Sales tests show whether people buy. Run these alongside the interviews. Every threshold below is a proposal. The founder should set and write down the final pass/fail lines before any data comes in.

A1 A2

Sample-to-purchase at a Happier Grocery demo

Sample at the store, and at a cheese counter as a comparison. Count samples given, same-visit purchases, and QR or email sign-ups.

Proposal: pass at 15% or more same-visit purchase among people who sampled; fail under 5%.

A2 A4

Price-ladder landing page

Paid social A/B test aimed at lookalikes of luxury-grocery shoppers. Singles at $7 / $10 / $14 and a gift trio at $24 / $32. Measure click-through and add-to-cart, not likes.

Proposal: pass if the $10 add-to-cart rate is at least 60% of the $7 rate, and the $32 trio is at least 60% of the $24 trio.

A3

60-day repeat tracking

Track demo and pop-up buyers individually by QR or email, with reorder checks at 30, 60 and 90 days. Report results separately for November–December and February–August.

Proposal: pass at 20–25% or more repeat at 60 days outside November–December; below 20% triggers a pivot to a gift-led brand.

A2 A3

Pre-order with deposit

Offer a refundable deposit to the waitlist and to interviewees. Split results by gift and self, and track subscription opt-in.

Proposal: pass if 10% or more of the waitlist places a deposit and at least a third of those are for themselves.
